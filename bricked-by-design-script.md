# Script: When Companies Turn Off Your Smart Devices (v3)

Shot tags: TH = talking head, Self = you record it, Online = brand or news footage, Screen = screen recording, Text = on-screen text.

The stand of this video: if a company sells you a product, it shouldn't be allowed to turn it into a paperweight. Re-check every date and number before recording.

---

## Opening (0:00–0:40)

**[TH: calm, like telling a friend a story. Gadget on the desk.]**
In 2016, people who owned a smart home hub called Revolv got an email from the company. From May 15, the app wouldn't open and the hub wouldn't work. Nothing was wrong with the hubs. The company had just decided to turn its servers off.

**[TH: straight to camera]**
I honestly never thought about this until I started researching this video. Then I found out it keeps happening, and most of the time it's completely legal. I'll go through a few cases, and by the end I think you'll agree with me on one thing: if a company sells you a product, it shouldn't get to turn it into a paperweight whenever it likes.

## The cases (0:40–2:50)

**[Online + Text: old Revolv promo clip. Caption: "$299 · May 15, 2016"]**
Revolv cost 299 dollars and came with what the company called a lifetime subscription. Nest, which is part of Alphabet, Google's parent company, bought it in 2014, stopped selling it straight away, kept it running for a while, and shut it down about two years later.

**[TH: straight to camera]**
So "lifetime" meant "until we get bored". Nest did end up giving full refunds, fair enough, but the hub was never broken. It just had nothing to talk to anymore.

**[Self: unplug a device, screen goes black. Can sit over the line above.]**

**[Online, Screen: Car Thing launch video, then Spotify's support page]**
Spotify's Car Thing, a 90 dollar music player for your car, stopped working in December 2024. Spotify's support page told owners to reset it and dispose of it as e-waste. Reset it, then bin it. Thanks for the music.

**[Online: AI Pin launch clip. Let this one breathe, it's the centerpiece.]**
Then there's the Humane AI Pin, which I think is the worst one. It launched at 699 dollars, plus a monthly subscription. In February 2025, after HP bought most of the company, it was cut off from its servers. Only people who'd bought it in the previous 90 days got their money back. So if you were on day 91, you'd paid 699 dollars for a very expensive lapel decoration.

Compare that with Revolv, where everyone got refunded. Same kind of shutdown, completely different outcome, and the only person who decided which one you got was the company.

**[Online: Wemo ads, one after another: plug, switch, heater, coffee maker, baby monitor]**
The newest one is from this year. On January 31, Belkin turned off the cloud for most of its Wemo products. That's more than two dozen of them, so the plugs and light switches, but also space heaters, coffee makers and baby monitors. Wemo had been around since 2011, so people had built a lot of their home around it. Yes, a baby monitor that needs a company's server to work.

**[Screen: Apple Home app. Then Online: Matter logo on a product box]**
Not every Wemo device died, though. A few models that work with Apple HomeKit carried on, but only if you'd set them up through HomeKit before the shutdown. And four newer models that use a standard called Matter weren't affected at all. Remember that word, I'll need it later.

**[Self: older smart TV at home or a relative's place, an app showing an error or missing from the home screen]**
Most of us here in India didn't buy these exact devices. But if you have an older smart TV where YouTube or Netflix has gone missing or throws an error, even though the screen is perfectly fine, that's the same thing, just smaller.

**[TH: point at the device on your desk on "in your house"]**
What I kept asking while reading all this was why these things need a company's server in the first place. It's in your house. It's on your own Wi-Fi. Why can't it just do its job?

## Why they do it (2:50–4:30)

**[Text: simple graphic. One payment for the device, then monthly server costs stacking up]**
Money is the obvious one. You pay once, when you buy the device, but the servers cost money every month, for every old device that's still online. After a few years those devices aren't bringing in anything new and the bill keeps coming.

**[Screen: scroll through subscription plan pages for smart cameras and doorbells]**
Subscriptions are the other big one. A monthly fee is steady income, which is why cameras, doorbells and pretty much everything else now comes with a plan. For some of these companies, the device is basically a very expensive sign-up form.

**[Screen: scroll a long terms page, highlight the "licensed, not sold" part]**
But the one that actually makes all this legal is the terms and conditions that nobody reads, me included. In a lot of them, the software inside your device isn't sold to you. It's licensed to you.

**[TH: hold the device]**
So what you actually own is the plastic, the glass and the circuit board. The smart part is on loan, and the company decides when to take it back. You bought a lamp, and the light is on a rental. At full price. That's the part I have a problem with.

**[Online: AI Pin footage again, the projected display if possible]**
Back to my question about working offline. After the shutdown, the AI Pin could still show its battery level. Calls, messages, all the AI features ran through Humane's servers, so they went with it. A 699 dollar gadget that could tell you its battery percentage, and nothing else.

**[TH: straight to camera]**
Now think about what a device that works fully offline means for the company. They get no data from you, they can't sell you a subscription for it, and you've got less reason to buy the new model. I don't think every company plans it that way, but it's not exactly a problem they're rushing to fix either.

## The bigger picture (4:30–6:00)

**[Self: walk past ACs, washing machines and TVs at Croma or Reliance Digital (ask staff first). Quick shot of you logging into an appliance app.]**
This matters more every year because software is in everything now. Cars, washing machines, ACs, TVs, electric scooters. A lot of them want an app and an account before they'll do their one job.

**[Online: car interior, the seats]**
A few years ago BMW tried charging a monthly fee for heated seats in some countries. The heating hardware was already in the car. People hated it and BMW dropped the idea, so complaining does work, apparently.

**[TH: straight to camera]**
There are some good things happening too.

**[Online, Screen: Matter logo, then Home Assistant's website]**
Those four Wemo devices that survived use Matter, a smart home standard that works with Google, Apple, Amazon and Samsung. Many Matter devices can be controlled locally, inside your home, with no company cloud in the middle. And there's a free piece of software called Home Assistant that lets you run your smart home on your own hardware.

**[Screen: the India Right to Repair portal homepage]**
Governments are paying attention too. India launched a Right to Repair portal in 2022, and the European Union has passed its own repair rules.

**[Self: your Pixel in hand]**
Even phones have improved. My Pixel is supposed to get seven years of updates, and not that long ago Pixels got three.

**[TH: straight to camera]**
But repair rules are mostly about fixing hardware, and this isn't a hardware problem.

Now, I get it. Nobody can run servers forever. That's actually why I think there should be a rule: if a company is going to switch off something it sold you, it should either refund you or let the device keep working without the cloud. And that's not far-fetched. Matter devices already work locally, and Home Assistant already lets people run it all themselves. If a company can't promise one of those two things, maybe it shouldn't be selling you a gadget that depends on it.

## Closing (6:00–7:00)

**[TH: straight to camera, normal pace]**
Until that changes, check before you buy. Ask what happens if the company shuts down. Can it still work locally? Does it support Matter? If it only works through the company's app and cloud, it can stop working long before the hardware wears out, and you'll have no say in it.
