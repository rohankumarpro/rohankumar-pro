## Opening (0:00–0:40)

In 2016, people who owned a smart home hub called Revolv got an email. The app would stop opening on May 15. The hub would stop working. Nothing was wrong with the hubs. The company just decided to switch off its servers.

Honestly, I never thought about this until I started researching this video. And then I found out it keeps happening. And most of the time? It's completely legal.

So let's go through a few of these cases, talk about why companies do it, and then what you can actually do about it.

## Real examples (0:40–2:30)

Let's start with Revolv. It cost 299 dollars, and it came with what the company called a *lifetime subscription*. Then in 2014, Nest bought it. Nest is part of Alphabet, which is Google's parent company. They stopped selling it right away, kept it running for a bit, and about two years later, they shut it down.

So "lifetime" meant exactly as long as the company felt like it. People were furious, and Nest ended up giving full refunds. But think about that: the hub itself was fine. Nothing inside it was broken. It just had nothing to talk to anymore.

And this keeps happening. Spotify's Car Thing, a 90 dollar music player for your car, stopped working in December 2024. And Spotify's support page told owners to reset it and dispose of it as e-waste. Just... throw it away.

Then in February 2025, the Humane AI Pin. 699 dollars, plus a monthly subscription. HP bought most of the company, and the servers got cut off. Only people who'd bought it in the last 90 days got their money back.

And the latest one is from this year. On January 31, Belkin turned off the cloud for most of its Wemo smart home products. That's more than two dozen products: smart plugs, light switches, space heaters, coffee makers, even baby monitors. Wemo had been around since 2011, so some people had built a big part of their home around it.

Now, not everything died. A few models that work with Apple HomeKit kept going, but only if you'd set them up through HomeKit before the shutdown. And four newer models that use something called Matter weren't affected at all. Remember that name, because it matters later.

Here in India, we mostly didn't buy these exact devices. But [YOUR STORY: an old smart TV, a gadget, or a relative's device where apps like YouTube or Netflix just stopped working even though the screen was fine]. It's the same problem, just smaller. The hardware works. The software moved on without it.

Now, the question I kept asking myself: why do these things even need a company's server? They're in your house, on your own Wi-Fi. Why can't they just work on their own?

## Why companies do this (2:30–4:30)

Part of the answer is money. When you buy a device, the company gets paid once. But servers cost money every single month, for every old device still online. After a few years, those old devices aren't bringing in any new revenue, but the company is still paying to keep them alive. At some point, someone in a meeting says "why are we doing this?"

Second, subscriptions. Companies love them because a monthly fee is steady income. That's why cameras, doorbells, and all kinds of gadgets now come with a plan. For some companies, the device is basically just the thing that gets you to sign up.

And third, and this is the sneaky one, the terms and conditions we all accept without reading. In a lot of them, the software in your device isn't *sold* to you. It's *licensed*.

So what you actually own is the hardware: the plastic, the glass, the circuit board. The software that makes it smart? That's something the company lets you use. And they decide when that stops.

Okay, back to my question about working offline. Some devices can do a little on their own. After the shutdown, the AI Pin could still show its battery level. That's it. Calls, messages, all the AI stuff went through Humane's servers, so all of it was gone.

And here's the uncomfortable part. If a device works fully offline, the company gets no data from you. They can't sell you a subscription. And you've got less reason to buy the next model. I'm not saying every company plans it this way, but the incentives point in one direction.

## The bigger picture (4:30–6:00)

This matters more every year, because software is in everything now. Cars, washing machines, ACs, TVs, electric scooters. A lot of them need an app and an account to unlock all their features.

A few years ago, BMW tried charging a monthly fee for heated seats in some countries. The heating hardware was already in the car. You'd paid for the car. People hated it, and BMW dropped the idea. Which tells you something: when people push back, companies do listen.

And there are some good things happening too.

Remember those four Wemo devices that survived? They use Matter, a smart home standard that works with Google, Apple, Amazon and Samsung, and many Matter devices can be controlled locally, inside your home, with no company cloud involved. There's also free software called Home Assistant that lets you run your whole smart home on your own hardware.

Governments are starting to care too. India launched a Right to Repair portal in 2022, and the EU has passed its own repair rules.

Even phones have gotten better. My Pixel is supposed to get seven years of updates. Not that long ago, Pixels got three.

## Closing (6:00–7:00)

So here's my one rule now. Before you buy a smart device, ask: what happens if this company shuts down or stops supporting it? Can it still work locally? Does it support Matter? If it only works through the company's app and cloud, you should know that before you pay, because it might stop working long before the hardware ever wears out.
